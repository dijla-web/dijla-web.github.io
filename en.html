The Tigris River and Sewage — Iraq's Lifeline in Crisis

The Tigris River, which nourished Sumerians, Assyrians, and Abbasids for thousands of years, faces an unprecedented crisis today. Not because of wars, but because of millions of cubic meters of untreated sewage pumped into it daily.

⚠️In April 2026, over 1,000 tons of fish died in the Tigris in a single event — caused by a discharge of untreated sewage that collapsed dissolved oxygen levels in the river.

What Exactly Is Happening?

The core problem is the lack of adequate sewage treatment infrastructure. Many residential areas, hospitals, and facilities discharge their wastewater directly into rivers without any treatment.

🏥Some hospitals near the Tigris discharge their medical waste and sewage into the river — adding dangerous pollutants including antibiotics and medical chemicals.

Impact on Children and Communities

Bacterial water pollution sharply raises rates of intestinal and skin diseases. Children are less able to resist bacterial pollutants as their immune systems are still developing.

A Regional Crisis: The Missing Water Diplomacy

Pollution isn't the only reason the Tigris is suffering — the river's sources lie outside Iraq's borders, in Turkey and Iran. Major dams built under projects such as Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project have sharply reduced the water reaching Iraq, in the absence of a binding international treaty regulating how Tigris and Euphrates water is shared between upstream and downstream countries.

🤝Iraq's Ministry of Water Resources warns that annual water inflows from the Tigris and Euphrates have fallen to less than half their historical levels, due to dams built by Turkey and Iran on the rivers' tributaries without a binding water-sharing agreement between the countries involved.
💡 Proposed Scientific Solutions
1

Sewage Treatment Plants

Build mandatory treatment plants before any river discharge. Baghdad opened seven units — expansion to all provinces is urgently needed.

2

Enforce Waste Treatment Regulations

Strict regulations requiring all healthcare and industrial facilities to treat their wastewater before disposal, with an effective monitoring system.

3

Partnership with UNICEF and UNEP

Collaborate with international organizations to fund clean water projects and build local capacity for sustainable water resource management.

4

Community Awareness

Awareness programs in schools and communities about clean water importance and protecting rivers from pollution.

5

International Water Diplomacy

Opening serious, direct negotiations with Turkey and Iran within binding international legal frameworks, to reach a fair agreement for sharing Tigris and Euphrates water that secures Iraq's historical share and reduces recurring seasonal drought crises.

Desertification in Iraq — The Black Land Turns to Sand

Iraq was historically known as the "Black Land" — for its abundant palms, trees, and fertile soil. Today, this land faces accelerating desertification, and official figures reveal the scale of the danger.

⚠️According to Iraq's Central Statistical Organization 2024: 96.5 million dunams are threatened, and 40.4 million have already desertified — 23% of Iraq's total area.

Why Is Iraq Desertifying?

Desertification results from intertwined natural and human factors. Natural causes include rising temperatures and scarce rainfall. The main human cause is reduction of water flowing through major dams on the Tigris and Euphrates.

🌊Dams on the Tigris and Euphrates have reduced water flowing into Iraq to just 20% of its historical volume — drying up agricultural areas that once fed millions.

Impact on Food and People

Iraq's productive agricultural area does not exceed 11.9 million dunams — insufficient for 42 million people. Desertification means less output, rising food prices, and displacement.

💡 Proposed Scientific Solutions
1

Green Belts and Afforestation

Plant drought-resistant trees on desert margins to create natural barriers preventing sand from encroaching on agricultural land.

2

Modern Water-Saving Irrigation

Transition to drip and sprinkler systems, saving 50-70% of water while increasing crop productivity.

3

International Water Diplomacy

Negotiate within international river water agreements for a fair water share guaranteeing continued agriculture.

4

Vegetation Restoration

Rehabilitate desertified land with drought-resistant local native plants capable of revitalizing the soil.

Pollution

Sulfur Pollution — A Wealth Burned in the Air

Iraq possesses enormous sulfur reserves ranking among the top globally. Yet few ask: where does this sulfur go? It is neither exported nor invested — it is burned in the air that citizens breathe daily.

🔴In 2003, a fire at the Mishraq Sulfur Factory near Mosul burned for about a month — officially recorded as the largest man-made release of sulfur dioxide in modern history.

How Does Sulfur Affect the Body?

When sulfur burns, sulfur dioxide (SO₂) rises. Upon contact with moisture, it converts to sulfuric acid — attacking the eyes and burning them, entering the lungs and damaging their tissue.

🔬Scientifically: SO₂ irritates the entire respiratory system, doubles asthma severity, weakens lung immunity, and affects the heart and nervous system with prolonged exposure.

Children — The Most Affected

A child's lungs are still developing and far more sensitive than an adult's. Brief exposure triggers severe asthma attacks. Children near oil fields suffer higher rates of respiratory diseases.

Wasted Wealth

Sulfur is a high-value industrial material used in fertilizers, batteries, rubber, and pharmaceuticals. Many countries import it at high prices. Iraq possesses huge quantities — but burns them instead of converting to national income.

💡 Proposed Scientific Solutions
1

International Environmental Standards

Adopt strict international standards in all oil fields and refineries, with continuous monitoring.

2

Convert Sulfur to Industrial Products

Build factories to convert raw sulfur into agricultural fertilizers and exportable industrial chemicals.

3

Air Quality Monitoring Network

Install SO₂ measurement stations near industrial areas to monitor pollution levels and issue early warnings.

4

Partnership with UNEP

Collaborate with the UN Environment Programme to create a comprehensive roadmap addressing industrial pollution.

The Mysterious Anbar Sinkhole — An Underground River and Rare Blind Fish, and How to Turn Them Into an Opportunity Instead of Harming Them

In the desert of Anbar, western Iraq, environmental police found a "khasfa" — a known sinkhole area — containing an underground river believed to be connected to the groundwater system of the Euphrates. The surprise was the presence of extremely rare, completely eyeless fish living in total darkness untouched by sunlight.

🔎The Anbar Environment Directorate clarified that calling it a "cave" is inaccurate — the area is locally known as a "khasfa," its level is higher than the Euphrates, and there is no direct leakage from the river. The water inside is entirely groundwater.

Why does this discovery matter?

These fish differ radically from open-water species; they are completely eyeless and feed on bacteria and decomposed organic matter carried by groundwater — a rare feeding pattern reflecting thousands of years of adaptation to a light-sealed environment. This time they were also recorded at larger sizes than old scientific records from 1955, opening the door to new research into this rare underground ecosystem.

A Parallel Risk: "Strange" Invasive Fish in the Euphrates

Alongside this discovery, videos have recently circulated showing quantities of African catfish being released into the Euphrates, raising concern among environmental activists and specialists over invasive species threatening native fish and disrupting the river's ecological balance. The reminder is important: any careless transport or release of fish — whether from fish farms or aquariums — into natural rivers can turn into a silent ecological disaster.

🐟Golden rule: never release any aquarium or farmed fish into a natural river or lake, no matter how harmless it seems — invasive species compete with native fish for food and can carry diseases the local ecosystem has no immunity against.
🛡️ How do we protect this underground treasure?
1

Designate the site as a protected reserve

Prevent random or unauthorized entry, and define a protected perimeter around the sinkhole to preserve the balance of its closed ecosystem.

2

Protect the groundwater from pollution

Ban dumping any waste, sewage, or chemicals near the site, since any pollution will reach the habitat of these sensitive creatures directly.

3

Conduct a comprehensive scientific survey before any intervention

Carry out integrated geological, hydrological, and biological studies to understand the extent of the passages and the species living there before allowing any investment.

4

Prevent the introduction of invasive species near the site

Completely avoid releasing any non-native fish or aquatic organisms near groundwater sources connected to the sinkhole.

🌱 How do we turn it into a useful scientific and tourism opportunity?
1

Guided, licensed scientific visits

Organize limited-capacity visits supervised by environmental and safety specialists, instead of allowing random entry that endangers both the site and visitors.

2

Scientific documentation that attracts international attention

Collaborate with universities and international environmental organizations to document the site and publish research, putting Anbar on the map of global scientific discoveries.

3

Responsible, limited eco-tourism

Develop a safe tourist route allowing visitors to view the site entrance without disturbing the internal ecosystem, with a nominal visit fee.

4

Reinvest proceeds into protection

Allocate proceeds from organized visits and international support to fund long-term maintenance and monitoring of the site instead of leaving it unprotected.

⚠️Important warning: do not attempt to enter the sinkhole or capture the fish yourself. Oxygen levels drop with depth, and specialized equipment plus trained teams are essential for any safe exploration. A single touch or sudden bright light could be enough to harm creatures adapted to total darkness for thousands of years.

The Amazon is the world’s largest tropical rainforest, stretching across nine South American countries and covering about 5.5 million square kilometers. It is often described as the “lungs of the Earth,” but deforestation from agriculture, cattle ranching, illegal logging, and road building is rapidly shrinking it.

In 2019 alone, the Amazon recorded more than 80,000 fires in a single season — a grim record that shows the pace of destruction. Scientists warn of a tipping point: if deforestation exceeds 25% of the forest, the whole region could shift to savanna.

Deforestation is not just the loss of trees; it releases huge amounts of carbon dioxide stored in biomass and soil, drives countless species toward extinction, and disrupts rainfall cycles across South America, Africa, and parts of Asia. Indigenous communities that have lived there for thousands of years also lose their home and culture.

The nine Amazon countries signed the Amazon Treaty in 1978 to cooperate on protection, but enforcement has remained weak. Brazil’s INPE monitors the forest with satellites and publishes daily data on lost forest area.

🌱 Proposed solutions worldwide:

Mass tree planting: The UN’s billion-tree initiative has already reached 14 billion trees. Trees absorb CO₂ and restore ecological balance.

Stop tropical deforestation: More than 140 countries pledged at COP26 to end deforestation by 2030. Making that real requires strict monitoring and international penalties.

Oceans cover 71% of Earth’s surface and contain 97% of the planet’s water. Yet humans have explored less than 20% of their depths. The deepest known point is the Mariana Trench in the Pacific, over 11,000 meters deep — deeper than the world’s tallest mountain is high. These depths still hold life forms that have never been seen or formally classified.

NOAA estimates that more than 91% of marine species have not yet been discovered. Every expedition returns with new species. Oceans also contain entire environments — hydrothermal fields, underwater salt lakes, and mountain chains longer than the Alps.

This unknown world faces mounting threats: plastic pollution, ocean acidification from excess atmospheric CO₂, and warming water that bleaches coral reefs and disrupts food chains. Coral reefs, which make up less than 1% of the ocean floor, support more than 25% of marine life.

Marine scientists estimate that industrial fishing has removed more than 90% of large fish from the oceans. UN reports say a third of the world’s fish stocks are exploited beyond biological limits, meaning fish populations are declining faster than they can recover.

🌱 Proposed solutions worldwide:

Marine protected areas: Protecting 30% of oceans by 2030 (COP15) gives marine life a chance to recover from overfishing and pollution.

Investing in ocean exploration: Increase funding for marine exploration so we can reach the 80% of oceans that remain unknown and document species before they vanish.

📚 Trusted reports and sources:

NOAA — State of the Global Oceans 2024 - National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration data on acidification, temperature, and pollution.

UNEP — State of Nature 2023 - A broad assessment of global biodiversity loss and ecosystem decline.

Climate change is the defining environmental challenge of the 21st century. The IPCC says current warming is extremely likely to be human-caused (>95%), mainly through fossil-fuel burning and deforestation. The highest atmospheric CO₂ levels before modern times date back to the Miocene, around 3 million years ago — meaning we are living in conditions humans have never experienced.

Iraq is among the countries most affected by global warming. Summer temperatures above 50°C have become common in Baghdad and Basra. The IPCC’s 2021 assessment classifies the Middle East and North Africa as a hotspot that will warm faster than the global average.

Rising heat does not just mean worse heatwaves — it shifts rainfall patterns, intensifies drought in places like Iraq and Africa, melts polar ice threatening countries such as Bangladesh and the Netherlands, and raises ocean acidity while fueling stronger storms and floods elsewhere.

Scientific projections warn of hundreds of millions displaced from low-lying coasts, 20–50% of species at risk of extinction by 2100 if warming continues, lower agricultural output, and more conflict over water and food.

Hope remains: renewable energy is growing faster than ever — solar and wind are now the cheapest power sources in many countries. The 2015 Paris Agreement united 196 countries around one goal, and initiatives such as regenerative agriculture and ocean protection can still make a real difference if implementation improves.

🌱 Proposed solutions worldwide:

Transition to renewable energy: Solar and wind are now the cheapest ways to generate electricity. Every country that speeds up the transition cuts emissions and reduces fossil-fuel dependence.

Environmental justice for developing countries: Industrialized countries fund adaptation projects in more vulnerable states like Iraq — as called for in the Paris Agreement, though implementation remains weak.
